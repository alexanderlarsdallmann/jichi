# Citing sources

Citation does two jobs: it gives credit (the integrity job) and it lets a reader
check and follow your sources (the scholarship job). Getting it right is not
bureaucracy — it is what makes your work trustworthy.

**What needs a citation:** any idea, fact, statistic, or wording that is not your
own or common knowledge. Common knowledge (the sky is blue; WWII ended in 1945)
does not; a specific figure, a scholar's argument, a particular phrasing does.
**When in doubt, cite** — over-citing is harmless, under-citing is plagiarism.

**Quote vs paraphrase — both need a citation:**

- **Quote**: the source's exact words, in quotation marks, with a citation.
- **Paraphrase**: the source's idea in *your* words, with a citation. Changing the
  wording does NOT make the idea yours — a paraphrase without a citation is
  plagiarism, and it is the most common honest mistake. Cite it.

**Two parts to a citation:**

1. **In-text** — a short pointer where you use the source (an author-date like
   `(Smith, 2020)`, a number like `[3]`, or a footnote, depending on the style).
2. **The reference list / bibliography** — the full entry at the end, so a reader
   can find the source.

**The major styles** (which one is set by your assignment — always check):

- **APA** (sciences, social sciences): author-date in text, "References" list.
- **MLA** (humanities): author-page in text, "Works Cited" list.
- **Chicago**: notes-and-bibliography (footnotes) or author-date, by field.
- **IEEE** (engineering): numbered `[1]` in text, numbered reference list.

Pick the required style and be **consistent** — consistent is more important than
memorized. For an exact format detail, consult the official style guide (or your
library's citation page) rather than guessing; the patterns are precise and the
details differ. A reference manager can help, but understanding *why* you cite is
what keeps you honest.
