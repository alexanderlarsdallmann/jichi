# The shape of an argument

Academic writing is not decorated opinion and it is not a summary — it is an
**argument**: a specific claim, defended with evidence, that a reasonable person
could disagree with. Get the shape right and clarity follows.

**The thesis — one arguable sentence.** The whole paper hangs on it. A thesis is:

- **Specific**, not a topic. Not "this essay discusses social media" — "social
  media's design incentives, not user weakness, drive compulsive use."
- **Arguable.** If nobody could disagree, it is not a thesis, it is a fact. "The
  sky is blue" is not a thesis; a claim someone could push back on is.
- **Yours to defend** for the length of the paper.

**The support — claims backed by evidence.** Under the thesis sit the claims that
prove it. Each claim needs:

- **Evidence** — data, a source, a text, an example — not just assertion.
- **Reasoning** — *why* the evidence supports the claim. Evidence does not speak
  for itself; you connect it.

The classic paragraph unit is **claim → evidence → analysis** (state it, show it,
explain why it matters). A paragraph that states without showing, or shows without
explaining, is half a paragraph.

**Answer the counter-argument.** A one-sided paper is weak. Acknowledge the
strongest objection to your thesis and respond to it — this is what separates a
confident argument from a naive one. It makes you *more* persuasive, not less.

**The frame — introduction and conclusion.** Write them last. The introduction
sets up the question and states the thesis; the conclusion says what the argument
established (and, honestly, its limits). Neither introduces new evidence.

**The test:** could a skeptical reader, after each paragraph, ask "says who?" or
"so what?" and find no answer? Where they could, the argument has a hole. Fill it
before you polish anything.
