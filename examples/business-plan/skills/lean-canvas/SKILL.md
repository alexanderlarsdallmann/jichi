# The lean canvas

*This describes a planning method, not business, financial, or legal advice.*

A traditional business plan is long, takes weeks, and is mostly wrong — because it
is full of confident guesses about a future nobody has tested. The **lean canvas**
(Ash Maurya's adaptation of the Business Model Canvas) fits the whole model on one
page, which forces honesty: you cannot hide a bad assumption in a phrase the way you
can in forty pages.

**The nine boxes:**

1. **Problem** — the top 1-3 problems your customer has (and what they do about them
   today).
2. **Customer segments** — who *specifically* has the problem; and *early adopters*,
   the narrow group who feel it most.
3. **Unique value proposition** — the single clear reason to care, in one line.
4. **Solution** — the top few features that address the problems (minimal).
5. **Channels** — how you reach customers.
6. **Revenue streams** — how you make money, and the price.
7. **Cost structure** — the main costs to deliver.
8. **Key metrics** — the few real numbers that show it is working.
9. **Unfair advantage** — what is hard to copy (often blank at first — that is
   honest).

**How to use it well:**

- **Every box is an assumption, not a fact.** Fill it in, then mark what you *know*
  versus *guess*. Most will be guesses; that is the point — the canvas surfaces them.
- **Keep it to phrases.** If a box needs a paragraph, you are hiding uncertainty.
- **Find the riskiest box.** Ask: which one, if wrong, kills the whole idea? Usually
  Problem + Customer ("do they have this problem and will they pay?"). That box is
  where your first cheap test goes.
- **Revise it as you learn.** The canvas is a living hypothesis. Each validation test
  updates a box. A canvas that never changes is one you are not testing.

Start here, on one page, before writing anything longer. The value is not the
document — it is seeing your assumptions laid bare so you can test the dangerous
ones cheaply.
