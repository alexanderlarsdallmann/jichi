# Synthesis, not summary

The most common literature-review mistake is a **serial summary**: "Author A found
X. Author B found Y. Author C found Z." Every source gets a paragraph, in the order
you read them, with no relationship shown. It reads as a list, and it wastes all
the thinking you did.

A **synthesis** is different in kind, not degree. It is organized by **idea**, and
it shows the *conversation* between sources.

**Summary (weak) vs synthesis (strong):**

- *Summary:* "Smith (2020) found remote work raised productivity. Jones (2021)
  found it lowered it. Lee (2019) found no effect." — three facts, no thinking.
- *Synthesis:* "The effect of remote work on productivity is contested: Smith
  (2020) reports gains and Jones (2021) losses, but the difference tracks their
  methods — Smith surveyed self-reports while Jones measured output — and Lee
  (2019) finds no effect once role type is controlled. The unresolved question is
  therefore not *whether* remote work helps but *for which roles*." — a conversation,
  and a gap.

**How to build one:**

1. **Organize by theme, not by paper.** Each section is a question or claim the
   field engages; multiple sources appear *within* it. (Your literature matrix
   columns become your sections.)
2. **Group and relate.** Within a theme, cluster sources that agree, present those
   that disagree, and — where you can — explain the disagreement (different method,
   population, definition).
3. **Add your judgment, marked as yours.** Weigh the evidence, note which position
   is better supported, keep your analysis clearly separate from the sources'.
4. **Build toward the gap.** A synthesis leads somewhere — to what is unresolved,
   contested, or unstudied. That gap is the opening for your own contribution; do
   not just trail off with "more research is needed."

**The test:** does your review read as a *discussion of ideas* in which sources are
evidence, or as a *tour of papers* one after another? If a reader could shuffle
your paragraphs and lose nothing, it is a summary. A synthesis has an argument, and
the order matters.
