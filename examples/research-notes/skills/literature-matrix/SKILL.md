# The literature matrix

A literature matrix (or "synthesis matrix") is a grid that organizes your reading
so you can *see* the field instead of holding it in your head. It is the single
best tool for moving from many summaries to one coherent understanding — and for
finding your gap.

**The shape:** a table with **sources down the rows** and **dimensions across the
columns.** Each cell says what that source contributes on that dimension.

```
| Source        | Question       | Method     | Key finding   | Theme A     | Theme B    |
|---------------|----------------|------------|---------------|-------------|------------|
| Smith 2020    | ...            | survey     | ...           | supports    | silent     |
| Jones 2021    | ...            | experiment | ...           | disputes    | central    |
| Lee 2019      | ...            | review     | ...           | supports    | qualifies  |
```

**Choosing columns:** always include the source's question, method, and key
finding (so you compare like with like). Then add a few **themes** — the recurring
questions or claims the field argues about. The right themes emerge as you read;
add columns as you notice them.

**What it reveals that a list cannot:**

- **Agreement** — a column where most sources line up (a settled point you can
  state briefly).
- **Disagreement** — a column where they conflict (a live debate — and where your
  judgment adds value).
- **Assumptions** — something every source takes for granted (often the most
  interesting thing to question).
- **The gap** — a cell that is empty across the board: a theme nobody addresses, a
  method nobody has tried, a population nobody has studied. *This is usually where
  your own contribution lives.*

**Using it:** fill it from your summaries (never invent a cell for an unread
source), then read *down the columns*, not across the rows — that is how the
patterns appear. The matrix is the bridge from reading to writing: your synthesis
is organized around the columns (themes), not the rows (sources).
