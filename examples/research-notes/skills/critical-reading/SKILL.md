# Reading a paper critically

Critical reading is not distrust — it is *engagement*. You read to understand AND
to judge: is this convincing, and where does it sit in the conversation? You do not
need to be an expert to do this; you need to ask the right questions.

**Read in passes, not linearly.** A useful order for a research paper:

1. **Skim first** — title, abstract, headings, figures, conclusion. Get the shape
   before the detail: what question, what answer.
2. **Read for the argument** — introduction (the question and why it matters) and
   conclusion (what they claim to have shown). Now you know what to look for.
3. **Read the method and results critically** — this is where belief is earned or
   lost.

**The questions that make reading critical:**

- **The question:** what problem, really? Is it the one the title implies?
- **The method:** does it actually address the question? Basic checks anyone can
  make — how big is the sample, how were things measured, could the design have
  shown them *wrong* (or was the answer baked in)?
- **The findings:** what did they actually find, stated plainly? Beware the gap
  between the results and the abstract's confident framing.
- **The limitations:** what does the paper admit — and what does it quietly not?
- **The fit:** does this agree with, extend, or contradict what you have read? A
  finding that stands alone is weaker than one others corroborate.
- **The interests:** who funded it, what did the authors expect to find? Not
  automatic disqualifiers, but context.

**The habit:** annotate as you go — questions in the margin, doubts, connections.
A paper you finished with a clean copy you did not really read; a paper covered in
your questions you engaged. And always separate, in your notes, what the paper says
from what *you* think — that honesty is the foundation of everything you write next.
