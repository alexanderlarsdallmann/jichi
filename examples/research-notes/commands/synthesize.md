description: Turn your matrix into a synthesis organized by IDEA, not by source — the opposite of a serial "Author A said, Author B said" summary.
---
Help the user write a **synthesis** of their sources (a literature-review section)
into `synthesis.md` — organized by *idea*, not by *source*. This is where the whole
reading process pays off, and where the #1 mistake lurks.

**The mistake to avoid:** a serial summary — "Author A said X. Author B said Y.
Author C said Z." That is organized by source, shows no relationships, and reads
as a list. A real synthesis is organized by **theme**, and shows the *conversation*
between sources.

Guide them to, from the matrix and summaries:

1. **Organize by theme, not by paper.** Each section is an idea/question the field
   engages, and multiple sources appear within it — grouped by what they say about
   *that* idea.
2. **Show the relationships.** Where sources agree, note the convergence; where they
   disagree, present the debate and (where the reader can) weigh it. "X and Y both
   find A, but Z argues B because..." — that is synthesis.
3. **Cite everything, honestly.** Every borrowed idea attributed; the reader's own
   analysis clearly theirs. Keep the two separate (this is the integrity line).
4. **Build toward the gap.** The synthesis should lead to what is unresolved or
   unstudied — the opening for the reader's own contribution — not just trail off.

The reader's judgment about how the sources relate is the actual scholarship; you
help them structure and express it, not supply the conclusions. Suggest the
`critical-reading-reviewer` to catch any section that slipped back into serial
summary, and the `academic-writing` bench when it becomes a full paper.
