# Buffer: the slack that saves the plan

The most common scheduling mistake is filling every hour. It feels productive — a
full calendar looks like a serious plan. But a wall-to-wall schedule has a fatal
flaw: **it assumes nothing goes wrong.** And something always does. One task runs
long, one interruption arrives, one morning your energy is low — and because there
is no slack, that overrun cascades into everything after it, and the whole day (or
week) collapses.

**Buffer is deliberately unscheduled time** that absorbs the inevitable surprises.
It is not laziness or wasted time — it is what makes the *rest* of the plan
reliable.

**How much:** leave roughly **20-30% of your available time unscheduled.** That
sounds like a lot until the first week a wall-to-wall plan falls apart by Tuesday.
The tighter the deadlines, the more buffer you need, not less — because there is
less room to recover.

**Where to put it:**

- **Daily**: gaps between blocks, and an unscheduled slot or two, so an overrun has
  somewhere to go without pushing over the next commitment.
- **Weekly**: keep part of a day (or several half-days) unplanned, for catch-up and
  the things that always come up.
- **Before deadlines**: schedule work to finish *before* the deadline with margin,
  not right against it. Finishing a day early is a gift to your future self;
  planning to finish at the last minute means any slip makes you late.

**The mindset shift:** a plan without buffer is not ambitious, it is fragile. Adding
buffer does not mean doing less — it means the things you *do* schedule actually get
done, because the plan survives contact with reality. Combined with honest
estimates (see `estimation`), buffer is what turns a hopeful schedule into one you
can trust.
