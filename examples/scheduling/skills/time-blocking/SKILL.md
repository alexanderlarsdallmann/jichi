# Time-blocking

A to-do list tells you *what*; a time-blocked day tells you *when*. That difference
is why time-blocking works: "work on the essay" is a hope, "9:00-10:30, essay
draft" is a plan you either keep or notice you broke. It converts intention into a
concrete commitment.

**How to block a day:**

1. **Give each task a specific block** with a start and end time. A good block is
   **60-90 minutes** — long enough to get into deep work, short enough that focus
   holds. Beyond ~90 minutes, most people fade; take a break and start a fresh
   block.
2. **Put deep work in your peak hours.** You are not equally sharp all day. Notice
   when you actually focus best (many are sharpest in the morning; some at night)
   and guard those hours for the hard, creative work. Do not spend your best two
   hours on email.
3. **Batch shallow work.** Email, admin, messages, errands — collect them into one
   or two blocks instead of letting them interrupt all day. Every context switch
   costs re-focus time.
4. **Protect the block.** A block only works if it is defended: notifications off,
   phone away, one task. A "focus block" full of interruptions is just a
   fragmented hour.
5. **Include breaks and transitions.** Real breaks between blocks (focus is a
   renewable resource, but only if you renew it), and a few minutes of transition —
   blocks scheduled back-to-back with no gap always bleed into each other.

**The honest limits:** three or four solid deep-work blocks is a *full* day for
most people — do not schedule eight. And do not block every minute; leave buffer
(see `buffer-planning`). A plan you follow at 80% beats a perfect grid you abandon
by mid-morning. Adjust the blocks to the day you actually had, and you get better
at it.
